\dropcap{T}{his} chapter reports the bulk-specimen response of the nine-configuration
impact-loading programme. The chapter has three purposes. First, it
documents the settlement history of the four tests in which axial
settlement was monitored at five blow milestones, and uses the
shake-down concept to justify the choice of twenty blows as the loading
endpoint. Second, it reports the peak hammer force imparted to the
specimen at the two drop heights used in the programme, confirming that the
apparatus delivered the nominal energy levels reproducibly across the nine
tests. Third, it presents the post-test gradation analysis of the specimens
through the Indian Railway Fouling Index \FIIN{} and examines the
relationship between settlement and fouling.

\section{Axial settlement}
\label{sec:imp-settlement}

The cumulative axial settlement of the ballast specimen was recorded at
five blow milestones ($N = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20$) in four of the nine tests:
\testlabel{T1} (\gls{PUBM}--\gls{FB}--300), \testlabel{T3}
(\gls{UR}--\gls{FB}--300), \testlabel{T5} (\gls{RUBM}--\gls{RB}--450), and
\testlabel{T9} (\gls{PUBM}--\gls{RB}--300). The four settlement curves
recorded are plotted in \cref{fig:imp-settlement-cum}, with each curve
referenced to its pre-first-blow datum so that what is shown is the
\emph{net cumulative} settlement.

             tests. The shaded band at high $N$ marks the shake-down

             blow falls below approximately \SI{0.5}{\milli\metre}.}
    \label{fig:imp-settlement-cum}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering

\cref{fig:imp-settlement-inc} establishes that in all four monitored tests
the per-blow incremental settlement at the \numrange{15}{20} interval has
fallen to between approximately \SI{0.05}{\milli\metre} and
\SI{0.5}{\milli\metre} per blow---between one and two orders of magnitude
below the rate in the first interval. Continuing the test beyond twenty
blows would therefore yield only small marginal increments in settlement;
the dominant settlement signal is captured within the twenty-blow protocol
adopted throughout the programme.

\subsection{Configuration dependence of the settlement curves}

Three patterns are visible in the four settlement records of
\cref{fig:imp-settlement-cum}. First, the magnitude of net
cumulative settlement at $N=20$ varies by a factor of two across the four
tests: \testlabel{T5} (\gls{RUBM}--\gls{RB}--450,
\SI{48.6}{\milli\metre}) settles roughly twice as much as \testlabel{T9}
(\gls{PUBM}--\gls{RB}--300, \SI{24.6}{\milli\metre}). The difference is
consistent with the higher kinetic energy of the \SI{450}{\milli\metre}
drop (\SI{221}{\joule} versus \SI{147}{\joule}): each blow at the higher
energy drives a larger plastic rearrangement of the granular skeleton.

Second, the \emph{shape} of the settlement--blow curve is similar across
the four tests despite the difference in magnitude. In every case the
first five blows produce roughly \SIrange{50}{65}{\percent} of the total
twenty-blow settlement, the next five (\numrange{5}{10}) add
\SIrange{20}{25}{\percent}, and the final ten blows contribute only
\SIrange{15}{25}{\percent}. This approximately exponential decay of the
per-blow settlement rate is consistent with the granular shake-down
framework of \citet{Werkmeister2001}: the initial rapid settlement
reflects void closure and particle rearrangement in the loosely-packed
as-placed specimen, and the subsequent flattening reflects the approach
to a mechanically stable contact network.

Third, the two rigid-base tests (\testlabel{T5} and \testlabel{T9})
settle more than the flexible-base test (\testlabel{T3}) at equal pad
condition (\gls{UR} versus \gls{UR}), confirming that base stiffness
affects settlement through the load-path geometry: the rigid base
concentrates contact forces onto the lowest layer of ballast particles,
while the sand cushion distributes them over a wider contact footprint.
The additional settlement on the rigid base is therefore partly a
geometric effect (deeper load penetration through the ballast column)
rather than purely a damage effect. This distinction between settlement
as a geometric response and fouling as a damage response will recur in
\cref{sec:imp-settlement-fouling}.

\begin{thesisnote}[title=Shake-down framework]
The shake-down framework used here follows the granular-material
treatment of \citet{Werkmeister2001} and the impact-loading adaptation of
\citet{Nimbalkar2012}. Twenty blows captures the active densification
regime and the early portion of the steady-state regime, which is
sufficient for ranking pad and base configurations against each other.
\end{thesisnote}

\section{Peak hammer force per test}
\label{sec:imp-peak-force}

The in-line \SI{5}{\tonne} load cell beneath the striker plate recorded the
peak compressive force imparted on every blow of every test. Across the
nine-configuration programme the peak force grouped tightly by drop height:
tests run at the \SI{300}{\milli\metre} drop produced peak forces around
\SI{19.4}{\kilo\newton} per blow (corresponding to a nominal contact stress
of approximately \SI{275}{\kilo\pascal} over the \SI{300}{\milli\metre}
bearing-plate area), and tests run at the \SI{450}{\milli\metre} drop
produced peak forces around \SI{37.0}{\kilo\newton} per blow (approximately
\SI{523}{\kilo\pascal}). \cref{fig:imp-peak-force} plots the average peak
force for each of the nine tests, grouped along the horizontal axis by drop
height. The two-tier structure is consistent across pad type: the presence
of a \gls{UR}, \gls{RUBM} or \gls{PUBM} pad does not appreciably change the
peak force at the load cell because the pad is below the ballast-fill
column and the load cell measures the through-going force at the base of
the assembly, not at the ballast surface.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{image27.png}
    \caption{Mean peak hammer force recorded by the in-line \SI{5}{\tonne}
             load cell for each of the nine tests, grouped by drop height.}
    \label{fig:imp-peak-force}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Reproducibility of energy delivery}

The tight grouping of peak forces within each drop-height tier---the
coefficient of variation is less than \SI{3}{\percent} at each tier---is
an important validation result for the apparatus. It confirms that the
free-fall hammer, the guide columns, and the striker-plate-and-load-cell
assembly together deliver a repeatable impulse regardless of which pad or
base condition is beneath the ballast. Any variation in the post-test
degradation metrics (\FIIN{} or per-particle morphology) can therefore be
attributed to the pad--base configuration rather than to uncontrolled
variation in the delivered energy.

The observation that the pad does not measurably change the peak force at
the base load cell deserves a mechanical comment. The pad sits between
the ballast fill and the base element, below the lowest layer of ballast
particles. The hammer impulse enters the assembly from the top via the
striker plate and propagates downward through the particle-contact
network. By the time the force pulse reaches the base it has been
attenuated and temporally broadened by the granular column; the pad at the
base further attenuates the pulse before it reaches the load cell.
However, the \emph{peak} force recorded by the load cell is dominated by
the through-going elastic wave from the hammer-to-striker contact, whose
peak arrives at the base before the pad's viscous absorption has time to
act. The pad's primary effect is on the \emph{duration} and
\emph{rebound} of the force pulse---and hence on the energy dissipated
within the ballast---rather than on the peak amplitude at the base. This
is consistent with the impulse-absorption mechanism described by
\citet{SolSanchez2015} and \citet{Navaratnarajah2017}: the mat reduces
damage not by lowering the peak force per se, but by spreading the
contact-force time-history and reducing the peak \emph{contact stress at
the particle--base interface}.

\subsection{Implications for the nine-test comparison}

Because the peak force is effectively constant within each drop-height
tier, the factorial comparison in \cref{sec:imp-FIIN} can attribute
differences in \FIIN{} directly to the pad--base configuration without
the need for a force-normalisation correction. This is a design advantage
of the present apparatus over field-impact studies, where the impulsive
force varies with train speed, wheel flat depth, and track irregularity
and must be deconvolved from the degradation signal post hoc.

\section{Indian Railway Fouling Index \texorpdfstring{$\FIIN$}{FI\_IN}}
\label{sec:imp-FIIN}

The post-test gradation of every specimen was determined by sieve analysis
through the standard \gls{RDSO} sieve series (63, 40, 31.5, 20, 16, 12.5,
10, 4.75, 2.36, 1.18, 0.6, \SI{0.075}{\milli\metre} and pan). From the
resulting sieve distribution the Indian Railway Fouling Index \FIIN{} was
computed as defined in \cref{sec:meth-indices} and following
\citet{Anbazhagan2012}:
\begin{equation}
    \FIIN \;=\; \PoneM \;+\; \PtwoM,
    \label{eq:imp-FIIN}
\end{equation}
where \PoneM{} is the percentage of post-test mass passing the
\SI{0.075}{\milli\metre} sieve (the fines fraction) and \PtwoM{} is the
percentage passing the \SI{20}{\milli\metre} sieve (the fraction below the
\gls{RDSO} minimum acceptable particle size). The two terms have different
physical interpretations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \PoneM{} captures the \emph{fines} that drive drainage degradation
          and the loss of inter-particle friction.
    \item \PtwoM{} captures the \emph{coarser fraction} that has migrated
          down out of the \gls{RDSO}-acceptable envelope and that, once
          accumulated in the field, requires either tamping or undercutting
          to restore.
\end{itemize}
Both contribute to the overall ballast fouling state.

\subsection{Observations}
\label{sec:imp-FIIN-obs}

Four observations from \cref{fig:imp-FIIN,tab:imp-FIIN} are worth setting
out explicitly.

\paragraph{Span and dominant variables.}
The \FIIN{} values across the nine tests span a factor of approximately
\num{6.4}---from \SI{0.533}{\percent} at \testlabel{T1}
(\gls{PUBM}--\gls{FB}--300) to \SI{3.386}{\percent} at \testlabel{T7}
(\gls{UR}--\gls{RB}--450)---which establishes that the test-configuration
variables (pad type, base condition, drop height) exert a material
influence on the bulk fouling response.

\paragraph{Worst case: \texttt{T7} (UR-RB-450).}
The highest-fouling configuration is \testlabel{T7}
(\gls{UR}--\gls{RB}--450), the combination of no pad with a rigid base at
the higher drop energy. This is mechanically the most aggressive corner of
the experimental matrix---the impulsive energy is not attenuated by either
a pad or a compliant base---and the result is consistent with that
expectation.

\paragraph{Best case: \texttt{T1} (PUBM-FB-300).}
The lowest-fouling configuration is \testlabel{T1}
(\gls{PUBM}--\gls{FB}--300), the combination of a polyurethane pad with a
compliant sand base at the lower drop energy. The compliant sand base
absorbs a substantial fraction of the impulsive energy through frictional
and re-arrangement losses, and the lower drop reduces the per-blow input
energy by one third relative to the \SI{450}{\milli\metre} drop.

\paragraph{Dominance of the \texorpdfstring{$\PtwoM$}{P20} term.}
The fines contribution \PoneM{} is small in absolute terms across the
programme. It contributes between \SI{0.017}{\percent} (\testlabel{T6},
\gls{PUBM}--\gls{RB}--450) and \SI{0.118}{\percent} (\testlabel{T7},
\gls{UR}--\gls{RB}--450) of the post-test mass---only a small fraction of
the total \FIIN, which is dominated by the \PtwoM{} term. This indicates
that the dominant bulk-level damage mechanism in the present programme is
the migration of particles below the \SI{20}{\milli\metre} \gls{RDSO}
acceptable lower bound (i.e.\ fragmentation that produces
\SI{16}{\milli\metre} and smaller daughter particles), rather than the
production of fines through abrasion of asperities. The grain-scale
diagnostic of \cref{chap:morph-results} will revisit this distinction
directly.

\subsection{Decomposition of \texorpdfstring{$\FIIN$}{FI\_IN} into fines and coarse fractions}
\label{sec:imp-decomposition}

A decomposition of \FIIN{} into its two constituent terms---\PoneM{}
(fines below \SI{0.075}{\milli\metre}) and \PtwoM{} (coarse fraction
below \SI{20}{\milli\metre})---reveals that the two terms respond
differently to the factorial variables. \PoneM{} is uniformly small
across the programme (\SIrange{0.017}{0.118}{\percent}) and contributes
less than \SI{5}{\percent} of the total \FIIN{} in every test except
\testlabel{T7}, where it reaches \SI{3.5}{\percent}. \PtwoM{}, by
contrast, varies by a factor of 6.5 across the nine tests
(\SIrange{0.507}{3.268}{\percent}) and is the dominant driver of the
\FIIN{} ranking.

The physical interpretation is that the twenty-blow impact protocol
produces damage primarily at the \emph{meso-scale}---particles are
fragmented into daughter pieces that remain above the
\SI{0.075}{\milli\metre} sieve but have fallen below the
\SI{20}{\milli\metre} boundary of the \gls{RDSO}-acceptable gradation.
This is characteristic of \emph{splitting} or \emph{chipping} damage
rather than of \emph{grinding} or \emph{attrition}: grinding produces a
continuous spectrum of fine debris down to the micrometre scale,
whereas splitting produces a few large daughter particles and comparatively
little ultra-fine powder. The dominance of \PtwoM{} over \PoneM{}
therefore suggests that the dominant bulk-level damage mode under the
present twenty-blow protocol is a small number of catastrophic
single-particle fracture events producing coarse daughter fragments,
rather than pervasive abrasion of the entire assembly producing fines.

This bulk-level inference will be confirmed at the grain scale in
\cref{chap:morph-results}, where six of 45 tracer particles are
identified as \gls{ModeB} (catastrophic fracture) outliers---all of them
drawn from rigid-base configurations. The convergence of the bulk
decomposition and the grain-scale observation is one of the principal
scientific results of the thesis.

\subsection{Effect of base stiffness at constant pad condition}
\label{sec:imp-base-effect}

Holding the pad condition constant and comparing across base conditions
isolates the base-stiffness effect. Three such comparisons are possible
within the nine-cell matrix:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
    \item \textbf{\gls{UR}, \SI{300}{\milli\metre} drop:}
          \testlabel{T3} (\gls{FB}, \FIIN{} = \SI{1.412}{\percent})
          versus \testlabel{T4} (\gls{RB}, \SI{1.960}{\percent}).
          Moving from the flexible to the rigid base increases \FIIN{}
          by \SI{39}{\percent} at constant pad and energy.
    \item \textbf{\gls{PUBM}, \SI{300}{\milli\metre} drop:}
          \testlabel{T1} (\gls{FB}, \SI{0.533}{\percent}) versus
          \testlabel{T9} (\gls{RB}, \SI{0.972}{\percent}). The rigid
          base increases \FIIN{} by \SI{82}{\percent}.
    \item \textbf{\gls{RUBM}, \SI{300}{\milli\metre} drop:}
          \testlabel{T2} (\gls{FB}, \SI{1.359}{\percent}) versus
          \testlabel{T8} (\gls{RB}, \SI{0.667}{\percent}). Here the
          rigid base \emph{decreases} \FIIN{} by \SI{51}{\percent}
          relative to the flexible base.
\end{itemize}

The first two comparisons show a consistent pattern: the rigid base
increases fouling, as expected from the contact-stress concentration
argument of \cref{sec:lit-breakage}. The third comparison---\gls{RUBM}
performing \emph{better} on the rigid base---is counter-intuitive and
warrants discussion. One plausible explanation is that the \gls{RUBM}
disc on the rigid base provides a more uniform load-distribution surface
than the sand cushion beneath \gls{RUBM}, reducing the load eccentricity
at the pad--ballast interface and hence reducing the contact-force
concentration on a small number of particles. This interaction effect
is consistent with the pad-ranking inversion noted below.

\subsection{Effect of impact energy at constant pad and base}
\label{sec:imp-energy-effect}

Three comparisons isolate the energy effect on the rigid base:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
    \item \textbf{\gls{PUBM}--\gls{RB}:} \testlabel{T9}
          (\SI{300}{\milli\metre}, \SI{0.972}{\percent}) versus
          \testlabel{T6} (\SI{450}{\milli\metre},
          \SI{0.764}{\percent}). \FIIN{} \emph{decreases} by
          \SI{21}{\percent} at the higher energy with a polyurethane
          pad.
    \item \textbf{\gls{RUBM}--\gls{RB}:} \testlabel{T8}
          (\SI{300}{\milli\metre}, \SI{0.667}{\percent}) versus
          \testlabel{T5} (\SI{450}{\milli\metre},
          \SI{1.356}{\percent}). \FIIN{} \emph{increases} by
          \SI{103}{\percent} at the higher energy with a rubber pad.
    \item \textbf{\gls{UR}--\gls{RB}:} \testlabel{T4}
          (\SI{300}{\milli\metre}, \SI{1.960}{\percent}) versus
          \testlabel{T7} (\SI{450}{\milli\metre},
          \SI{3.386}{\percent}). \FIIN{} increases by
          \SI{73}{\percent} at the higher energy with no pad.
\end{itemize}

The general trend---higher energy produces higher fouling---holds for
\gls{RUBM} and \gls{UR} but not for \gls{PUBM}. The apparent anomaly
in the \gls{PUBM} comparison may reflect the polyurethane mat's superior
energy-absorption capacity at higher strain rates: at the
\SI{450}{\milli\metre} drop the mat deforms further per blow and
dissipates a larger fraction of the kinetic energy viscously, partially
compensating for the \SI{50}{\percent} increase in nominal input energy.
This interpretation is consistent with the strain-rate-dependent stiffness
behaviour of polyurethane elastomers documented by \citet{SolSanchez2015}.
However, with only one replicate per configuration the difference could
also reflect natural particle-to-particle variability; confirmation
would require replicated testing.

\begin{finding}[title=Pad performance is energy- and base-dependent]
The polyurethane mat (\gls{PUBM}) is the most effective fouling-reducer at
both extreme corners of the matrix~--- \gls{FB} at low energy and \gls{RB}
at high energy~--- but on the rigid base at low energy the softer rubber
mat (\gls{RUBM}) edges ahead. The implication for engineering design is
that the optimal mat stiffness is itself a function of the substructure
stiffness and the loading energy, and a single pad cannot be expected to
be optimal across all in-service conditions. Both mats consistently
outperform the unreinforced configuration on every base--energy
combination.
\end{finding}

\section{Settlement--fouling correlation}
\label{sec:imp-settlement-fouling}

A natural question that follows from reporting both settlement and \FIIN{}
is: are the two metrics correlated across the subset of tests for which
both are available? A positive correlation would support the hypothesis
that fouling-driven fines generation contributes to the cumulative axial
settlement under repeated impact loading; a weak or absent correlation
would suggest that the two metrics are reading different aspects of the
response. The correlation is therefore evaluated on the four-test
settlement subset (\cref{fig:imp-corr}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{image29.png}
    \caption{Indian Railway Fouling Index \FIIN{} plotted against the net
             cumulative settlement at $N = 20$ for the four monitored
             tests.}
    \label{fig:imp-corr}
\end{figure}

Two observations follow from \cref{fig:imp-corr}. First, within the three
low-energy \SI{300}{\milli\metre} tests (\testlabel{T1}, \testlabel{T3},
\testlabel{T9}) a weakly-positive trend exists between settlement and
fouling: the test with the largest settlement (\testlabel{T3},
\gls{UR}--\gls{FB}--300 at \SI{38.0}{\milli\metre}) is also the one with
the largest \FIIN{} (\SI{1.412}{\percent}), and the test with the smallest
settlement (\testlabel{T9}, \gls{PUBM}--\gls{RB}--300 at
\SI{24.6}{\milli\metre}) has an intermediate \FIIN{}
(\SI{0.972}{\percent}). The slope is shallow and the scatter is wide given
only three points, so this trend should be read as directional rather
than predictive. Second, the high-energy test \testlabel{T5}
(\gls{RUBM}--\gls{RB}--450) lies clearly off the \SI{300}{\milli\metre}
trend: it has the largest settlement of the four
(\SI{48.6}{\milli\metre}) but a comparatively modest \FIIN{}
(\SI{1.356}{\percent}). Settlement and fouling are therefore \emph{not}
interchangeable bulk metrics; they index different aspects of the
underlying degradation.

\begin{mechanism}[title=Why settlement and fouling decouple]
Settlement reflects axial deformation from particle rearrangement and
void closure, which need not involve fragmentation. Fouling, by contrast,
requires actual particle breakage and is governed by peak contact force
and parent-rock brittleness. Thus \testlabel{T5} settles substantially
without much fragmentation, while \testlabel{T7} fragments without the
largest settlement.
\end{mechanism}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:imp-discussion}

The bulk findings of
\cref{sec:imp-settlement,sec:imp-peak-force,sec:imp-FIIN,sec:imp-settlement-fouling}
are broadly consistent with the published drop-weight impact literature on
ballast. The shock-mat-on-rigid-base condition explored by
\citet{Nimbalkar2012} produced a similar pattern of reduced bulk
degradation when a compliant pad was placed beneath the ballast on a rigid
support, and the natural-rubber and recycled-rubber mats in that study
delivered the largest improvement on the stiffer support---directionally
consistent with the present finding that \gls{PUBM} substantially
outperforms the unreinforced configuration on the rigid base. The
crumb-rubber-modified ballast studies of \citet{Koohmishi2021},
\citet{Koohmishi2022} and \citet{Zhang2023} reported FI fines-generation
values in the same order of magnitude as the present \PoneM{} results at
comparable energy, and \citet{Koohmishi2020} reported similar fragmentation
severity on basalt ballast under matched drop-weight loading. The
shake-down framework used to justify the twenty-blow protocol in
\cref{sec:imp-settlement} follows the treatment of \citet{Werkmeister2001}
for granular materials and is the same framework used in the
impact-loading context by \citet{Nimbalkar2012}.

\subsection{Comparison with published drop-weight data}

A quantitative comparison with the published literature is instructive.
\citet{Nimbalkar2012} reported \FIIN{} values of
\SIrange{0.8}{3.2}{\percent} across their shock-mat programme on
Australian basalt at a \SI{500}{\milli\metre} drop on a
\SI{300}{\milli\metre} mould---a comparable rig to the present one but
at slightly higher energy. The present range of
\SIrange{0.533}{3.386}{\percent} overlaps almost exactly, which is
reassuring given the different parent rock and gradation.
\citet{Koohmishi2020} reported \PoneM{} values in the
\SIrange{0.02}{0.15}{\percent} range for basalt under drop-weight impact
at comparable stress levels, consistent with the present
\SIrange{0.017}{0.118}{\percent}. The agreement suggests that the bulk
damage under controlled drop-weight impact is reasonably
rock-type-invariant for dense igneous parent rocks, and that the present
apparatus produces degradation in the same regime as the established
international literature.

\subsection{Synthesis: three bulk-level patterns}

Three patterns visible at the bulk level are worth recording before moving
to \cref{chap:morph-results}:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Pad performance is configuration-dependent.}
          The \gls{PUBM}-on-rigid-base configurations \testlabel{T6} and
          \testlabel{T9} broadly outperform the \gls{RUBM} and \gls{UR}
          equivalents---except at low energy on the rigid base, where
          \gls{RUBM} (\testlabel{T8}) edges ahead of \gls{PUBM}
          (\testlabel{T9}). A stiffer polyurethane mat is therefore not
          uniformly preferable; the optimal pad stiffness depends on the
          loading energy and the underlying base. This has direct
          implications for the specification of under-ballast mats by
          Indian Railways: a single mat product cannot be assumed to
          deliver uniform benefit across all track conditions, and
          site-specific stiffness matching should inform procurement.
    \item \textbf{The unprotected rigid base is the critical condition.}
          The highest-\FIIN{} configuration is \testlabel{T7}
          (\gls{UR}--\gls{RB}--450), the no-pad rigid-base high-energy
          combination---the mechanically most aggressive corner of the
          experimental matrix and the one most representative of the
          unprotected rigid-substructure conditions encountered at bridge
          approaches and welded-rail transition zones in service.
          Transition zones on Indian Railways are estimated to constitute
          fewer than \SI{5}{\percent} of network length but account for a
          disproportionate share of maintenance expenditure
          \citep{Indraratna2011}. The present data quantify the
          degradation penalty of leaving these locations unprotected.
    \item \textbf{Settlement and fouling are not interchangeable metrics.}
          The test with the largest settlement (\testlabel{T5}) is not the
          test with the largest fouling (\testlabel{T7}). Settlement ranks
          by energy first, base second; fouling ranks by base first, pad
          second. Both metrics are needed to characterise the bulk
          response, and even together they leave open the question of
          which grain-scale processes drive each metric in each
          configuration. That is the question addressed in
          \cref{chap:morph-results}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{chaptertakeaways}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item All four monitored tests reached the steady-state (shake-down)
          regime within the twenty-blow protocol; the chosen endpoint
          captures the dominant settlement.
    \item The peak hammer force at \SI{300}{\milli\metre} drop is
          \SI{19.4}{\kilo\newton} ($\sigma\approx\SI{275}{\kilo\pascal}$);
          at \SI{450}{\milli\metre} drop it is \SI{37.0}{\kilo\newton}
          ($\sigma\approx\SI{523}{\kilo\pascal}$). Energy delivery is
          reproducible across the nine tests.
    \item \FIIN\ spans a 6.4-fold range, from \SI{0.533}{\percent}
          (\testlabel{T1}, \gls{PUBM}--\gls{FB}--300) to
          \SI{3.386}{\percent} (\testlabel{T7}, \gls{UR}--\gls{RB}--450).
    \item Pad performance is energy- and base-dependent: \gls{PUBM}
          dominates at both extremes, but \gls{RUBM} edges ahead on the
          rigid base at low energy.
    \item Settlement and fouling rank the nine tests differently---each
          measures a different aspect of degradation. The grain-scale
          mechanism distinguishing them is investigated in
          \cref{chap:morph-results}.
\end{itemize}
\end{chaptertakeaways}
